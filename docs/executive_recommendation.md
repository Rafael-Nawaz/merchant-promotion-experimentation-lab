# Executive Recommendation

**Engagement:** Merchant Promotion Experimentation Lab
**Question:** Should the 3% Dining + Entertainment cashback promotion be expanded, narrowed, changed, or stopped?
**Basis:** Randomised controlled test, 10,000 cardholders, 12 weeks pre / 12 weeks post, ~448,000 transactions. Causal effect estimated by difference-in-differences on eligible-category spend.

---

## Bottom line

> **Narrow the promotion.** Run it only for **Affluent Frequent** and **Digital
> Native** customers. Remove it from **Mainstream**, **Budget Occasional** and
> **Dormant Reactivation**, where it either loses money or produces no measurable
> lift. Targeting these two segments turns a marginal, uncertain program
> (blended ROI ≈ 30%, confidence interval crossing zero) into a clearly positive
> one (**ROI ≈ 49%, net benefit ≈ $17k per 12-week window on the 3,370 tested
> customers**) and removes the cashback spend that destroys value.

---

## What the experiment found

**The promotion works, on average - but only just, and not reliably.**

* Treatment customers spent **$91.75 more per head** over the 12 weeks than the
  control trend implies (95% CI $3.85–$179.66, p = 0.04).
* On the cleaner primary outcome - Dining + Entertainment spend - the effect is
  **$49.23 per customer and highly significant** (p < 0.001).
* The parallel-trends placebo test passes (pre-period placebo DiD = −$5.30,
  p = 0.81), so the design is sound.
* Blended across all tested customers, ROI is **+30%**, but the confidence
  interval runs from **−30% to +89%** - the data cannot rule out that the program
  as currently run loses money.

**The average hides a wide spread across segments.**

| Segment | Eligible-spend lift | Statistically significant? | ROI (identified) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Digital Native** | +24.5% | Yes (p < 0.001) | **+64%** | **Target / expand** |
| **Affluent Frequent** | +20.7% | Yes (p < 0.001) | **+43%** | **Target / expand** |
| Mainstream | +10.1% | Yes (p = 0.009) | **−23%** | Keep out - lift is real but below break-even |
| Budget Occasional | +12.1% | Marginally (p = 0.03) | **−10%** | Keep out |
| Dormant Reactivation | +0.9% | No (p = 0.93) | **−93%** | Keep out - no measurable response |

Break-even is a **~14% lift in eligible spend** (3% cashback ÷ 25% margin, net of
cashback paid on incremental spend). Only Affluent Frequent and Digital Native
clear it. Mainstream's 10% lift is genuine but the cashback paid on their large
base of *inframarginal* spend costs more than the margin the extra spend earns.

**Heavier spenders respond more.** Within every segment, customers in the top two
pre-period spend quintiles show a DiD of ~$160 vs. ~$35 for the bottom quintile.
Targeting should skew to higher-spend cardholders even inside the two
recommended segments.

**No regional pattern.** Regional DiD estimates are all positive but none is
individually significant; the promotion is not a regional play.

**Halo is promising but unproven.** Point estimates show meaningful spillover
into other categories (e.g. +$105/customer for Digital Native), which would push
ROI far higher. But this test was not sized to measure it and the halo estimates
are not individually reliable. Do not bank on it - test it.

## Recommendation detail

### 1. Target these segments
- **Affluent Frequent** and **Digital Native**.
- Within them, prioritise the top ~40% of customers by prior Dining +
  Entertainment spend.
- Expected economics at national scale (≈253k customers, 4 windows/year), base
  case: **+$122M incremental eligible spend, $20M cashback cost, +$10M annual net
  benefit, +49% ROI.** Conservative case is roughly break-even (−9%); upside case
  is +107%.

### 2. Do not extend the promotion to
- **Mainstream** - real but sub-break-even lift. Revisit only with a cheaper or
  tiered offer (see below).
- **Budget Occasional** - small lift, negative ROI.
- **Dormant Reactivation** - no measurable spend response. If the goal for this
  group is reactivation rather than incremental spend, that needs a different
  offer and a different success metric (reactivation rate, not spend lift), not
  this promotion.

### 3. Changes to test next
- **A lower or tiered cashback rate (e.g. 2%, or 3% only above a spend
  threshold)** for Mainstream. Their lift may survive a cheaper offer that then
  clears break-even.
- **A dedicated halo test** - larger sample or longer window - to confirm the
  cross-category spillover. If it holds, the targeted ROI is closer to +150%.
- **A scaled confirmatory rollout** for the two recommended segments before full
  national launch, to tighten the confidence interval (the conservative case is
  still slightly negative).

### 4. Do not
- Roll the promotion out broadly "because the average is positive." The average
  is positive but statistically fragile, and most of the cashback budget in a
  broad rollout would fund Mainstream and Budget Occasional spend that either
  wouldn't move or wouldn't pay back.

---

## Business rationale

Cashback is a blunt instrument: it pays every eligible customer for spend they
were mostly going to make anyway, and only a fraction of the payout buys genuinely
new spend. That trade works only where the behavioural response is large relative
to the existing spend base - in this test, affluent and digitally engaged
customers with elastic discretionary budgets. For everyone else the promotion is
a transfer, not an investment. Narrowing the program concentrates the cashback
budget on the customers who actually change their behaviour, moving ROI from a
marginal, statistically fragile +30% to a solid +49%.
